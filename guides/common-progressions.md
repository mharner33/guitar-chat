# Common Progressions

A handful of chord progressions account for an enormous share of popular, folk, blues and jazz music. Each one is a short path through a key's diatonic chords, and each is written in Roman numerals so the same path can be played in any key.

This guide gives each progression in Roman numerals, shows it in a key that is comfortable on guitar with open shapes, and explains why it works. All tab is written from string 6 (low E) to string 1 (high E).

## I–IV–V

The three major chords of a major key, built on degrees 1, 4 and 5. I–IV–V in G major is G – C – D.

- G: `3-2-0-0-0-3`
- C: `x-3-2-0-1-0`
- D: `x-x-0-2-3-2`

Between them, these three chords contain every note of the major scale, so they can harmonize almost any simple melody in the key. The progression moves from home (I) to a predominant chord (IV) to the dominant (V), which pulls back to I. Countless folk, country and rock songs use nothing else. Common orderings include I–IV–V–I, I–IV–I–V and I–V–IV–I.

## ii–V–I

The backbone of jazz harmony, usually played with 7th chords. ii–V–I in C major is Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7.

- Dm7: `x-x-0-2-1-1`
- G7: `3-2-0-0-0-1`
- Cmaj7: `x-3-2-0-0-0`

Each root falls a fifth to the next (D to G, G to C), which is the strongest root motion in tonal music. The voice leading is smooth as well: the 7th of each chord falls a semitone or stays put to become the third of the next. C, the 7th of Dm7, falls to B, the third of G7; F, the 7th of G7, falls to E, the third of Cmaj7. Jazz standards chain ii–V–I patterns through many keys, so recognising the shape is one of the most useful listening skills a guitarist can build.

## I–V–vi–IV

A four-chord loop heard in a vast number of pop songs. I–V–vi–IV in C major is C – G – Am – F.

- C: `x-3-2-0-1-0`
- G: `3-2-0-0-0-3`
- Am: `x-0-2-2-1-0`
- F: a barre chord at the 1st fret, or a simplified four-string grip

The loop never settles for long. It leaves home, reaches the dominant, then sidesteps to vi instead of resolving, and IV leads back round to I. Starting the same loop on vi (vi–IV–I–V, Am – F – C – G) gives it a more minor, wistful flavour without changing a single chord.

## The 12-bar blues

The 12-bar blues uses I, IV and V, but plays all three as dominant 7th chords, which is a blues convention rather than something the key's diatonic chords supply. In A, the chords are A7, D7 and E7.

- A7: `x-0-2-0-2-0`
- D7: `x-x-0-2-1-2`
- E7: `0-2-0-1-0-0`

The standard layout, one chord per bar:

| Bars 1–4 | Bars 5–8 | Bars 9–12 |
|---|---|---|
| I I I I | IV IV I I | V IV I V |
| A7 A7 A7 A7 | D7 D7 A7 A7 | E7 D7 A7 E7 |

The form is three lines of four bars. The first line states home, the second moves to IV and back, and the third climbs to V, steps down through IV and returns to I. The final bar's V is the turnaround: it sets up bar 1 again so the form can repeat. On the last time through, players usually stay on I in bar 12 to end.

A common variation, the quick change, plays IV in bar 2 (A7 D7 A7 A7 for the first line). Shuffle and swing rhythms suit the form, and the A blues scale or A minor pentatonic fits over all three chords.

## Minor i–iv–v

The minor-key version of I–IV–V. In A natural minor, i–iv–v is Am – Dm – Em.

- Am: `x-0-2-2-1-0`
- Dm: `x-x-0-2-3-1`
- Em: `0-2-2-0-0-0`

All three chords are minor, so the progression has a dark, modal colour and a fairly soft return home, because Em has no leading tone. To strengthen the cadence, raise the third of the v chord: Em becomes E major or E7, borrowing G# from the A harmonic minor scale. The progression Am – Dm – E7 – Am has the decisive pull of a major-key V–I while keeping the minor tonic.

## Using these progressions

Learn each progression by its numerals, not its letters. Once you know that ii–V–I means "minor chord on 2, dominant on 5, home on 1", you can find it in any key by counting up the scale. Try each one in at least two keys, for example I–IV–V in both G and A, and listen for the function of each chord rather than its name.
