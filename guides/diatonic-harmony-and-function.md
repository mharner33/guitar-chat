# Diatonic Harmony and Function

A key is more than a scale. When you build a chord on every note of a scale using only that scale's notes, you get a family of seven chords that belong together. These are the diatonic chords of the key, and nearly every song in a major or minor key draws most of its chords from that family.

Knowing the family is half the picture. The other half is function: the role each chord plays, whether it feels like home, like leaving home, or like a strong pull back. This guide builds the chords of C major, labels them, and explains why the V chord resolves to I.

## Harmonizing the major scale

To harmonize a scale, stack thirds on each degree using only notes from the scale. In C major (C D E F G A B), the chord on C takes C, E and G; the chord on D takes D, F and A; and so on up to B, which takes B, D and F.

Because the scale has an uneven pattern of whole and half steps, the thirds that land on each degree differ. Some chords get a major third at the bottom and some a minor third, and one gets a diminished fifth. The result is always the same pattern of qualities for every major key: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished.

## Roman numerals

Roman numerals name chords by scale degree rather than by letter, so a progression can be described once and played in any key.

- Upper case (I, IV, V) means a major chord.
- Lower case (ii, iii, vi) means a minor chord.
- Lower case with a degree sign (vii°) means a diminished chord.

A 7 after the numeral adds the diatonic 7th: V7 in C is G7, ii7 is Dm7.

## The chords of C major

| Roman | Chord | Notes | 7th chord | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | C | C E G | Cmaj7 | tonic |
| ii | Dm | D F A | Dm7 | predominant |
| iii | Em | E G B | Em7 | tonic |
| IV | F | F A C | Fmaj7 | predominant |
| V | G | G B D | G7 | dominant |
| vi | Am | A C E | Am7 | tonic |
| vii° | Bdim | B D F | Bm7b5 | dominant |

The same seven numerals apply in every major key. In G major, for instance, the chords are G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em and F#dim.

## Tonic, predominant, dominant

Function groups the seven chords into three roles.

**Tonic (I, iii, vi)** chords feel stable. I is home itself; iii and vi share two notes with I and can stand in for it, which is why a song can land on vi for a softer, less final arrival.

**Predominant (ii, IV)** chords move away from home and prepare the dominant. They contain the 4th degree, F in C major, which is one of the two notes that will create tension in the dominant.

**Dominant (V, vii°)** chords create the strongest pull back to the tonic. Both contain B, the leading tone a semitone below C, and both can carry the tritone described below.

The typical journey is tonic → predominant → dominant → tonic, for example I–IV–V–I or I–ii–V–I. Music can break that order freely, but the pattern explains why so many progressions sound right.

## Why V resolves to I

V to I is the strongest motion in tonal harmony, and the dominant 7th shows why. G7 is spelled G B D F. Two of its notes, B and F, are a tritone apart: 6 semitones, exactly half an octave. The tritone is the most unstable interval in the key, and it resolves in a very specific way.

- B, the leading tone, moves up a semitone to C.
- F, the 7th of the chord, moves down a semitone to E.

C and E are the root and third of the C major chord, so the tension in G7 collapses directly into the tonic triad. Meanwhile the bass falls a fifth from G to C, the strongest root movement there is. Together, the semitone resolutions and the falling fifth make V7–I sound conclusive.

vii° does the same job with less weight. Bdim (B D F) contains that same B–F tritone but lacks the G root, which is why it is grouped with V as a dominant chord.

## The same ideas in minor

Harmonizing A natural minor (A B C D E F G) gives i, ii°, III, iv, v, VI and VII: Am, Bdim, C, Dm, Em, F and G. This corpus labels them tonic (i, III), predominant (ii°, iv, VI), dominant (v) and subtonic (VII).

The weak point is v. Em has G, a whole step below A, so there is no leading tone and no tritone to resolve. Minor-key music usually fixes that by raising G to G#, borrowed from the A harmonic minor scale. The minor v becomes E major, or E7 (E G# B D) with the D–G# tritone, and the pull to Am becomes as strong as V7–I in major.
