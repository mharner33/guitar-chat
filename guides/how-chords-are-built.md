# How Chords Are Built

Almost every chord in tonal music comes from one simple procedure: start on a root and stack notes a third apart. Three notes give a triad, four give a 7th chord, and continuing upward gives 9ths, 11ths and 13ths. Once you know which kind of third goes where, chord symbols stop being a list to memorise and become instructions you can read.

All the examples here are built on C so they can be compared directly. Every formula works the same way from any root.

## Stacking thirds

Take the C major scale, C D E F G A B, and pick every other note starting from C: C, E, G. That skip-one pattern is stacking thirds. Continue it and you get B, then D, F and A in the next octave. Each new note is a third above the last one, and the chord's name tells you how far up the stack to go and which thirds are major or minor.

A major third is 4 semitones and a minor third is 3 semitones. The order of those two building blocks decides the chord quality.

## The four triad qualities

| Triad | Formula | Thirds (bottom, top) | Spelling |
|---|---|---|---|
| C major | 1 3 5 | major + minor | C E G |
| C minor | 1 b3 5 | minor + major | C Eb G |
| C diminished | 1 b3 b5 | minor + minor | C Eb Gb |
| C augmented | 1 3 #5 | major + major | C E G# |

Major and minor triads both contain a perfect fifth, which makes them stable. Diminished narrows the fifth to a tritone, and augmented widens it by a semitone; both are restless and tend to lead somewhere else.

On guitar: C major is `x-3-2-0-1-0`, and C minor can be played as the barre shape `x-3-5-5-4-3`.

## Seventh chords

Add one more third on top of a triad and you have a 7th chord. Five types cover most music:

| Chord | Built from | Formula | Spelling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cmaj7 | major triad + major 7th | 1 3 5 7 | C E G B |
| C7 | major triad + minor 7th | 1 3 5 b7 | C E G Bb |
| Cm7 | minor triad + minor 7th | 1 b3 5 b7 | C Eb G Bb |
| Cm7b5 | diminished triad + minor 7th | 1 b3 b5 b7 | C Eb Gb Bb |
| Cdim7 | diminished triad + diminished 7th | 1 b3 b5 bb7 | C Eb Gb Bbb |

The plain "7" symbol always means the dominant 7th: a major triad with a minor 7th on top. It is the chord the 5th degree of a major key produces naturally, and its built-in tritone (E to Bb in C7) is what makes it want to resolve. Cm7b5 is also called half-diminished. In Cdim7 the 7th is written Bbb because it is the 7th letter above C lowered twice; it sounds the same as A.

Guitar shapes: Cmaj7 `x-3-2-0-0-0`, C7 `x-3-2-3-1-3`, Cm7 `x-3-1-3-1-3`.

## Sixth chords

A 6th chord adds the 6th degree to a triad instead of a 7th. C6 is C E G A and Cm6 is C Eb G A. Both use a major 6th (A) above the root. The 6th gives a sweet, settled colour without the pull of a 7th, which is why C6 often works as a final chord. A C6 shape on guitar is `x-3-2-2-1-3`.

## Extensions: 9, 11 and 13

Keep stacking thirds past the 7th and the new notes land beyond the octave, so they are named 9, 11 and 13. They are the 2nd, 4th and 6th degrees placed an octave higher.

- C9 = C E G Bb D (dominant 7th plus a 9th)
- Cmaj9 = C E G B D (major 7th plus a 9th)
- C11 = C E G Bb D F
- C13 = C E G Bb D A

An extension number implies the dominant 7th underneath unless the symbol says "maj". In practice the 11th is usually left out of a 13th chord, because F clashes with the major third E a semitone below it.

Six strings cannot hold every note of a large chord, so guitarists drop the least important tones first. The fifth goes first, then the root if a bass player is covering it. The third and 7th stay because they define the quality. A common C9 shape, `x-3-2-3-3-3`, keeps root, third, 7th, 9th and fifth on the top five strings.

## Suspended chords replace the third

A sus chord removes the third and puts a 2nd or 4th in its place:

- Csus2 = C D G (formula 1 2 5)
- Csus4 = C F G (formula 1 4 5)

With no third, a sus chord is neither major nor minor. That ambiguity is its sound: open, hovering and slightly unresolved. Csus4 in particular tends to move to C major, with F falling a semitone to E. Shapes: Csus2 `x-3-0-0-1-3`, Csus4 `x-3-3-0-1-1`.

A sus chord is different from an add chord. Cadd9 keeps the third and adds the 9th (C E G D), so it stays clearly major.
