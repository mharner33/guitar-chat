# Why Major and Minor Sound Different

Most listeners can tell a major chord from a minor chord within a second, even with no training. The surprising part is how small the physical difference is. A major triad and a minor triad built on the same root share two of their three notes; only one note moves, and it moves by a single semitone. That note is the third.

This guide explains what changes between major and minor, first in a single chord and then across a whole scale, and separates the parts that come from acoustics from the parts that come from listening habits.

## The third decides the quality

Measure up from the root of a chord to its third. If the distance is 4 semitones, the interval is a major third (C up to E). If the distance is 3 semitones, it is a minor third (C up to Eb). Everything else about a basic triad can stay the same: the fifth sits 7 semitones above the root in both cases.

- C major triad: C E G (major third, then minor third on top)
- C minor triad: C Eb G (minor third, then major third on top)

Both triads span the same perfect fifth from C to G. Inside that fifth, the third is either placed high (major) or low (minor). The outer frame is identical; only the inner note shifts.

You can hear this on guitar with one finger. Play an open A major chord, `x-0-2-2-2-0`, then change it to A minor, `x-0-2-2-1-0`. The only thing that moved is the note on string 2: fret 2 is C#, the major third of A, and fret 1 is C, the minor third. One fret on one string turns the whole chord from bright to dark.

## C major and C natural minor side by side

A scale has more than one third in it, so the difference between a major key and a minor key is larger than the difference between two chords. Lay the two scales out from the same root:

| Degree | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C major | C | D | E | F | G | A | B |
| C natural minor | C | D | Eb | F | G | Ab | Bb |

C major scale: C D E F G A B. C natural minor scale: C D Eb F G Ab Bb.

Four degrees are shared (1, 2, 4 and 5) and three are lowered by a semitone in minor: the 3rd, the 6th and the 7th. Written as formulas, major is 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 and natural minor is 1 2 b3 4 5 b6 b7. The step patterns show the same thing from another angle: major runs whole–whole–half–whole–whole–whole–half, while natural minor runs whole–half–whole–whole–half–whole–whole.

The b3 is the note that makes the tonic chord minor. The b6 and b7 are what make the whole key feel minor rather than just its home chord.

## What the b6 and b7 add

**The b6.** In C natural minor the 6th degree is Ab, only a semitone above G. A note a semitone above the 5th tends to lean down onto it, so melodies in minor often feature that Ab-to-G sigh. The b6 also changes the chords of the key: the chord on the 4th degree becomes F minor (F Ab C) instead of F major, and the chord on the 6th degree becomes Ab major.

**The b7.** In C major, B sits a semitone below C. That half step is called the leading tone, and it creates a strong pull up to the root. In natural minor the 7th is Bb, a whole step below C, so the pull is much weaker. The chord on the 5th degree also becomes minor (G Bb D) instead of major, and a minor v chord does not push toward home as hard as a major V chord does.

Composers often want the dark colour of minor and the strong pull of the leading tone at the same time. The common fix is to raise only the 7th, which gives the harmonic minor scale: C D Eb F G Ab B. That one change restores the leading tone and makes the 5th-degree chord major again.

## Consonance, tension and the physics part

Some of the major/minor difference is acoustic. When two notes sound together, their frequencies combine, and simpler frequency ratios produce smoother, more stable blends. A perfect fifth is close to a 3:2 ratio, a major third close to 5:4, and a minor third close to 6:5. All three count as consonant, but the fifth is the most stable of them, which is why both triads share it as their frame.

A major triad also lines up neatly with the natural overtones of its root. Any pitched note contains faint higher partials, and the 4th, 5th and 6th of those partials form a major triad. A minor triad does not appear that low in the overtone series, so it is a slightly more complex blend. That is a real, measurable difference, and it helps explain why major often sounds settled and minor sounds a little more shaded.

What physics does not supply is tension in the strong sense. Neither triad is dissonant. The real tension in tonal music comes from intervals such as the tritone or a semitone clash, and from expectations such as a leading tone waiting to resolve.

## "Sad" is a learned association

Minor is commonly heard as darker or sadder than major, and in Western music that association is strong and reliable. It is not, however, a law of acoustics. Physics explains why the two triads blend differently; it does not explain why one particular blend should mean sorrow.

Several observations point to learning rather than hard-wiring. Young children tend to judge a tune's mood from its tempo and loudness before they respond reliably to major versus minor. Plenty of fast, bright, danceable music is written in minor keys, and plenty of slow, mournful songs are in major. Tempo, rhythm, register, lyrics and performance style often outweigh mode when listeners describe how music feels.

The practical takeaway for a guitarist: the third gives you a colour, and the 6th and 7th extend that colour across a key. Whether the result sounds sad, tense, tender or triumphant depends on everything else you do with it.
