# Modes and Their Chords

A mode is what you get when you keep the notes of a scale but treat a different note as home. The seven modes of the major scale all use exactly the same seven pitches; they differ only in which note acts as the root, and that changes the pattern of whole and half steps heard from that root.

Because the notes are shared, the chords are shared too. The question that matters for a guitarist is not "which chords belong to Dorian?" but "which chord do I lean on so the music sounds Dorian rather than plain major?" This guide answers that for every mode of C major.

## Modes are rotations of one scale

Start with C major (C D E F G A B) and begin on each note in turn, keeping all seven notes and no accidentals:

| Mode | Notes |
|---|---|
| C Ionian | C D E F G A B |
| D Dorian | D E F G A B C |
| E Phrygian | E F G A B C D |
| F Lydian | F G A B C D E |
| G Mixolydian | G A B C D E F |
| A Aeolian | A B C D E F G |
| B Locrian | B C D E F G A |

C Ionian is simply the C major scale under its modal name, and A Aeolian is the A natural minor scale. Every row is the same set of white-key notes. What changes is the step pattern measured from the first note, and that step pattern is what your ear hears as the mode's flavour.

## Formulas and characteristic notes

Comparing each mode with the major or natural minor scale on the same root shows which degrees are altered. The single most distinctive alteration is the mode's characteristic note: the note that, if you emphasise it, makes the mode unmistakable.

| Mode | Formula | Family | Characteristic note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ionian | 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 | major | (the reference) |
| Dorian | 1 2 b3 4 5 6 b7 | minor | natural 6 |
| Phrygian | 1 b2 b3 4 5 b6 b7 | minor | b2 |
| Lydian | 1 2 3 #4 5 6 7 | major | #4 |
| Mixolydian | 1 2 3 4 5 6 b7 | major | b7 |
| Aeolian | 1 2 b3 4 5 b6 b7 | minor | b6 |
| Locrian | 1 b2 b3 4 b5 b6 b7 | diminished | b5 |

In D Dorian the characteristic note is B, the natural 6 that natural minor would flatten. In E Phrygian it is F, a semitone above the root. In F Lydian it is B, the raised 4th. In G Mixolydian it is F, the flattened 7th. In A Aeolian it is F, the b6. In B Locrian it is F, the flattened 5th that makes the tonic chord diminished.

## The seven shared 7th chords

Stacking thirds on each note of C major produces seven 7th chords. Every mode of C major owns all seven of them:

| Root | Chord | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| C | Cmaj7 | C E G B |
| D | Dm7 | D F A C |
| E | Em7 | E G B D |
| F | Fmaj7 | F A C E |
| G | G7 | G B D F |
| A | Am7 | A C E G |
| B | Bm7b5 | B D F A |

That is the reason modal playing confuses people at first: a song in D Dorian and a song in C major draw on an identical chord pool. The difference is not the inventory but the centre of gravity. The chord you start on, return to and end on tells the ear which note is home.

## The home chord for each mode

Each mode's home chord is the 7th chord built on its first degree:

| Mode | Home chord | A partner chord that shows off the characteristic note |
|---|---|---|
| C Ionian | Cmaj7 | Fmaj7 or G7 |
| D Dorian | Dm7 | G7 (contains B, the natural 6) |
| E Phrygian | Em7 | Fmaj7 (rooted on F, the b2) |
| F Lydian | Fmaj7 | G7 (contains B, the #4) |
| G Mixolydian | G7 | Fmaj7 (rooted on F, the b7) |
| A Aeolian | Am7 | Fmaj7 (rooted on F, the b6) |
| B Locrian | Bm7b5 | Cmaj7 (rooted on C, the b2) |

A simple way to establish a mode is a two-chord vamp: the home chord plus a partner that contains the characteristic note. Dm7 to G7 and back sounds Dorian because the B in G7 keeps reminding the ear of the natural 6 over a D minor centre. G7 to Fmaj7 sounds Mixolydian because the F chord spells out the b7 against a G major centre.

Be careful with dominant-function chords. In D Dorian, the G7 is fine as a colour, but if you follow G7 with Cmaj7 the ear hears a V–I cadence into C and the music slides back to Ionian. Modal vamps usually avoid that resolution.

Locrian is the odd one out. Its home chord, Bm7b5, contains a diminished fifth (B to F), which is unstable on its own, so Locrian rarely works as a long-term home. It shows up more as a passing colour, for example over the ii chord of a minor key.

## Modes in other keys

The formulas travel. C Dorian is 1 2 b3 4 5 6 b7 built on C, which spells C D Eb F G A Bb. Those are the notes of Bb major, because C is the 2nd degree of Bb major, just as D is the 2nd degree of C major. To find the parent scale of any mode, count back to the major scale it rotates from: Dorian sits on degree 2, Phrygian on 3, Lydian on 4, Mixolydian on 5, Aeolian on 6 and Locrian on 7. The home chord follows the same rule, so C Dorian's home chord is Cm7.
