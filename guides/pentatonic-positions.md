# Pentatonic Positions

The minor pentatonic scale is the first scale most guitarists learn for soloing, and for good reason. It has only five notes, no semitone steps to clash with the chords, and it fits the fretboard in five compact shapes called positions, or boxes. Each position covers about four or five frets, with two notes on every string.

This guide uses A minor pentatonic throughout, because its first position sits in the middle of the neck at the 5th fret. The shapes are movable: slide every fret number up or down by the same amount to play the scale in any other key.

## The formula: 1 b3 4 5 b7

The minor pentatonic takes the natural minor scale and removes its 2nd and b6. What remains is the formula 1 b3 4 5 b7. Built on A, that gives the A minor pentatonic scale: A C D E G.

Leaving out B and F removes both of the semitone steps in A natural minor (B to C and E to F). Every step in the pentatonic is either a whole step or a minor third (three semitones), which is why almost any note sounds acceptable over an A minor chord and why the scale is so forgiving for improvisers.

## The five positions

Frets are listed per string, string 6 (low E) to string 1 (high E). Each string has two notes in each position.

| Position | Fret range | Str 6 | Str 5 | Str 4 | Str 3 | Str 2 | Str 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5–8 | 5, 8 | 5, 7 | 5, 7 | 5, 7 | 5, 8 | 5, 8 |
| 2 | 7–10 | 8, 10 | 7, 10 | 7, 10 | 7, 9 | 8, 10 | 8, 10 |
| 3 | 9–13 | 10, 12 | 10, 12 | 10, 12 | 9, 12 | 10, 13 | 10, 12 |
| 4 | 12–15 | 12, 15 | 12, 15 | 12, 14 | 12, 14 | 13, 15 | 12, 15 |
| 5 | 14–17 | 15, 17 | 15, 17 | 14, 17 | 14, 17 | 15, 17 | 15, 17 |

**Position 1 (frets 5–8)** is the classic box. Its lowest note is the root A at string 6 fret 5, and A appears again at string 4 fret 7 and string 1 fret 5. Nearly every rock and blues player starts here.

**Position 2 (frets 7–10)** starts on C at string 6 fret 8. **Position 3 (frets 9–13)** starts on D at string 6 fret 10 and has the widest stretch, on string 2. **Position 4 (frets 12–15)** starts on E at string 6 fret 12; it is the open-position box (open strings plus frets 1–3) moved up an octave. **Position 5 (frets 14–17)** starts on G at string 6 fret 15 and leads back to position 1 one octave higher, at fret 17.

Notice that each position's lowest note on string 6 is the next scale tone after the previous position's: A, C, D, E, G. The positions climb the scale.

## Neighbouring positions share notes

The positions are not separate shapes; they overlap. The higher note on each string of one position is the lower note on that string in the next position. For example, position 1's upper frets (8, 7, 7, 7, 8, 8 from string 6 to string 1) are exactly position 2's lower frets.

The same holds all the way up: position 2's upper notes are position 3's lower notes, position 3's upper notes are position 4's lower notes, and position 4's upper notes are position 5's lower notes. Position 5's upper notes, all at fret 17, are position 1 again one octave up.

Because of this overlap, moving between positions is a matter of shifting your hand by one note on each string, not jumping to an unrelated shape. Practising the scale across two neighbouring positions at once is the quickest way to stop feeling trapped in one box.

## The relative major: same notes, new home

The C major pentatonic scale is C D E G A, the same five notes as A minor pentatonic in a different order. The two scales are relatives, just as C major and A natural minor are relatives: A minor's root is three semitones below C.

That means the five positions above are also the five positions of C major pentatonic. Only the root you emphasise changes. Over an A minor chord or groove, target A, C and E; over a C major chord, target C, E and G and the same box sounds bright instead of bluesy. Many players call A minor position 2 "C major position 1", because it begins on C at string 6 fret 8.

## The blues scale adds the b5

The blues scale is the minor pentatonic plus one passing note, the b5. The A blues scale is A C D Eb E G, with formula 1 b3 4 b5 5 b7. The Eb sits a semitone between D and E and is usually played as a quick chromatic step or a bend rather than a note to rest on.

In position 1 the added Eb falls at string 5 fret 6 and string 3 fret 8, one fret beyond the D on each of those strings. Adding those two frets to the position 1 box gives the most common blues scale shape on the guitar.
